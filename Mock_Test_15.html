The Discovery of Insulin

Before the early 1920s, a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes was effectively a death sentence, with patients, particularly children, typically surviving only a short time after onset, often managed solely through extremely restrictive low-carbohydrate diets that could delay but not prevent the disease's fatal progression.

The breakthrough came in 1921 at the University of Toronto, where surgeon Frederick Banting, working alongside medical student Charles Best under the supervision of physiologist John Macleod, successfully isolated insulin from the pancreas of dogs, demonstrating that injections of this extracted substance could effectively lower blood sugar levels in diabetic animals.

The team's first human trial occurred in January 1922, when 14-year-old Leonard Thompson, critically ill with diabetes, received an insulin injection. Although the initial injection caused an allergic reaction due to impurities in the extract, a purified version developed shortly afterward with the help of biochemist James Collip produced dramatic improvement, and Thompson went on to live for over a decade afterward, a previously unimaginable outcome for someone with his condition.

Recognizing the discovery's enormous humanitarian significance, Banting and Macleod were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1923, though the decision proved controversial, as Best and Collip, who had both made substantial contributions, were initially excluded from the award—a decision Banting publicly protested by sharing his prize money with Best.

In a decision now widely regarded as remarkably significant, the discoverers sold the patent for insulin to the University of Toronto for a symbolic sum of one dollar, explicitly intending to ensure the treatment would remain affordable and widely accessible rather than becoming a source of personal profit. Insulin has since transformed diabetes from a swiftly fatal condition into a manageable chronic illness, though access and affordability challenges persist in various parts of the world today.

Ocean Currents and Climate

The world's oceans are crisscrossed by an extensive system of currents that move vast quantities of water across the globe, driven by a combination of wind patterns, differences in water density caused by temperature and salinity variation, and the Earth's rotation, which deflects moving water through a phenomenon known as the Coriolis effect.

Among the most significant of these systems is thermohaline circulation, sometimes referred to as the global 'conveyor belt,' a deep-ocean circulation pattern driven primarily by differences in water density. In this system, cold, salty, and therefore dense water sinks in polar regions, particularly the North Atlantic, before slowly flowing along the ocean floor and eventually resurfacing elsewhere through a process that can take centuries to complete a full circuit.

One particularly well-known surface current, the Gulf Stream, transports warm water from the Gulf of Mexico along the eastern coast of North America before crossing the Atlantic toward Western Europe, significantly moderating the climate of regions such as the United Kingdom and northern Europe, which experience considerably milder winters than other locations at similar latitudes lacking comparable warm current influence.

Ocean currents play a role extending well beyond simply redistributing heat; they also influence weather patterns, support marine ecosystems by transporting nutrients, and affect fishing industries that depend on predictable current-driven patterns of marine life distribution. Disruptions to these systems can therefore carry consequences extending considerably beyond ocean temperatures alone.

Scientists have expressed growing concern regarding the potential effects of climate change on ocean circulation patterns, particularly thermohaline circulation. Increased freshwater input from melting polar ice could, according to some climate models, reduce water density in critical sinking regions, potentially weakening or disrupting this circulation system—a change that could paradoxically contribute to regional cooling in parts of Europe even as global average temperatures continue to rise, representing one of several complex and sometimes counterintuitive ways climate change may manifest regionally.

PASSAGE 3

The History of Writing Systems

Writing stands among humanity's most transformative inventions, enabling the recording and transmission of information across time and distance in ways that spoken language alone cannot achieve. The earliest known writing systems emerged independently in several regions, with Sumerian cuneiform in Mesopotamia, developed around 3400 BCE, generally regarded as among the oldest, alongside Egyptian hieroglyphics, which emerged at a broadly similar period.

Early cuneiform began not as a system for recording language directly, but rather as a practical accounting tool, using simple pictographic symbols pressed into clay tablets to track agricultural goods, trade transactions, and administrative records. Over several centuries, these symbols gradually evolved from direct pictorial representations into more abstract wedge-shaped marks, eventually developing the capacity to represent spoken language more fully, including grammatical elements and abstract concepts.

A particularly significant development in writing's history was the emergence of alphabetic systems, in which a relatively small number of symbols represent individual sounds rather than entire words or syllables. The earliest alphabetic systems are generally traced to the Phoenician civilization, around 1200 BCE, whose relatively simple 22-symbol alphabet proved remarkably adaptable and influential, eventually giving rise, through various adaptations, to the Greek, Latin, and numerous other alphabets used widely around the world today.

Not all writing systems adopted purely alphabetic principles. Chinese writing, for instance, developed along a different trajectory, relying on logographic characters that represent whole words or meaningful units rather than individual sounds, a system that has remained in continuous use, with significant evolution, for over three thousand years, representing one of the world's oldest continuously used writing systems.

The invention of writing carried implications extending far beyond simple record-keeping, fundamentally transforming how societies could organize administratively, preserve and transmit accumulated knowledge across generations, and develop complex legal, religious, and literary traditions. Many historians consider the emergence of writing a defining marker separating prehistoric societies from historical ones, given writing's unique capacity to preserve detailed records of past events for future study.
