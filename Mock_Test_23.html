The History of Eyeglasses

Eyeglasses, now so commonplace that billions of people worldwide wear them daily, have a history stretching back roughly seven centuries, making them one of the oldest vision-correcting technologies still in widespread use. Before their invention, people with poor eyesight had few options, and conditions such as farsightedness often made detailed work, including reading and manuscript copying, difficult or impossible later in life.

The earliest eyeglasses are generally believed to have been invented in northern Italy in the late 13th century, with the first references to them appearing in written records around 1290. These early devices consisted of two magnifying lenses made of polished quartz or glass, mounted in frames of wood, bone, or metal and joined at a hinge, designed to be held in front of the eyes or balanced on the nose rather than worn on the ears.

For several centuries, eyeglasses remained relatively expensive and were associated primarily with scholars, monks, and wealthy individuals. Early lenses were convex, helping those with farsightedness, and it was not until the 15th century that concave lenses designed to correct nearsightedness were developed, a significant improvement that extended the benefits of eyeglasses to a wider range of people.

The invention of the printing press in the 15th century substantially increased demand for eyeglasses, as the spread of printed books created a growing population of readers who needed vision correction. Temples, the arms that rest over the ears, were not introduced until the 18th century, finally allowing glasses to stay securely in place without needing to be held or balanced.

In the modern era, eyeglass technology has advanced considerably, with lightweight plastic lenses, anti-reflective coatings, and progressive lenses that correct multiple vision problems within a single lens. Contact lenses and laser eye surgery now offer alternatives, yet eyeglasses remain by far the most widely used form of vision correction worldwide, a testament to the enduring practicality of a seven-hundred-year-old invention.

Wetlands and Flood Control

Wetlands, areas where water covers the soil or is present near the surface for all or part of the year, include marshes, swamps, bogs, and mangrove forests. Once widely regarded as wastelands to be drained for farming or development, wetlands are now recognized as among the most valuable ecosystems on Earth, providing services worth trillions of dollars annually.

One of the most important functions of wetlands is flood control. Wetland soils and vegetation act like natural sponges, absorbing large quantities of rainfall and river overflow and then releasing it slowly over time. This reduces the peak height of floods and protects downstream communities. Coastal wetlands, particularly mangroves and salt marshes, additionally buffer shorelines against storm surges and wave energy.

Wetlands also improve water quality by acting as natural filters. As water moves slowly through wetland vegetation, sediments settle out and plants absorb excess nutrients and pollutants, including nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff. Some constructed wetlands are now deliberately built to treat wastewater, offering a lower-cost and lower-energy alternative to conventional treatment plants.

In addition, wetlands support remarkable biodiversity. Although they cover only a small fraction of the planet's surface, they provide habitat for a disproportionately large number of species, including migratory birds, fish, amphibians, and countless invertebrates. Many commercially important fish species depend on wetlands as nursery habitat during the early stages of their lives.

Despite these benefits, wetlands continue to disappear. Estimates suggest that the world has lost roughly a third or more of its wetlands since the early 1700s, with drainage for agriculture and urban expansion the leading causes. Conservation efforts, including international agreements such as the Ramsar Convention and restoration projects that reconnect rivers with their floodplains, aim to reverse this trend, though progress remains uneven across regions.

PASSAGE 3

The Psychology of Music

Music is found in every known human culture, and archaeological evidence, including bone flutes dating back more than 40,000 years, suggests that it has accompanied humanity for a very long time. This universality has led psychologists and neuroscientists to ask a fundamental question: why do humans make and enjoy music, and what does it do to the brain?

Brain imaging studies show that listening to music activates a wide network of regions, including those involved in hearing, movement, memory, and emotion. Unlike many other activities, music engages both hemispheres of the brain simultaneously. When people listen to music they find deeply pleasurable, scans reveal the release of dopamine, the same neurotransmitter associated with other rewarding experiences such as eating and social bonding.

Music's emotional power is closely tied to memory. Many people find that a particular song can instantly recall vivid memories of a specific time and place. Researchers have also found that music can remain accessible to people with advanced dementia, who may no longer recognize family members yet can still sing familiar songs, a finding that has led to growing use of music-based therapy in care settings.

Beyond emotion, learning to play an instrument appears to bring cognitive benefits. Studies comparing musicians and non-musicians have found differences in brain structure, particularly in areas controlling fine motor skills and auditory processing. Some research suggests that musical training in childhood may be associated with improved language skills and working memory, although researchers debate whether music causes these advantages or whether children who are already skilled are simply more likely to take up music.

Music is also a powerful social force. Group singing, drumming, and dancing synchronize the movements and emotions of participants, and studies suggest that such shared musical activities can strengthen feelings of trust and belonging within a group. Some evolutionary theorists propose that this social bonding function may explain why music became so deeply embedded in human societies in the first place.
