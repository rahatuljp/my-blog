The Discovery of Electricity

Electricity powers nearly every aspect of modern life, yet its practical use is barely two centuries old. The ancient Greeks were aware of static electricity as early as 600 BCE, when the philosopher Thales of Miletus observed that amber, when rubbed with fur, could attract light objects such as feathers. The Greek word for amber, elektron, eventually gave rise to the word electricity.

Serious scientific study began in the 17th and 18th centuries. In 1600, English physician William Gilbert distinguished between magnetic and electrical effects and coined the term electricus. In 1752, the American statesman and scientist Benjamin Franklin conducted his famous kite experiment during a thunderstorm, helping to demonstrate that lightning is a form of electricity, an insight that later led to the invention of the lightning rod.

A major step toward practical use came in 1800, when Italian physicist Alessandro Volta invented the voltaic pile, the first true battery, which produced a steady flow of electric current. Until then, scientists had only been able to study brief electrical sparks from static charge. Volta's invention gave researchers, for the first time, a reliable and continuous source of electricity to experiment with.

In the 1830s, English scientist Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction, showing that moving a magnet near a coil of wire generates an electric current. This principle is the foundation of the electric generator and the transformer, and it made large-scale production of electricity possible. Faraday had received little formal education, yet his experimental brilliance made him one of the most influential scientists in history.

The late 19th century saw electricity move from the laboratory into everyday life. Thomas Edison developed a practical incandescent light bulb in 1879 and built early power stations, while Nikola Tesla championed alternating current, which could be transmitted over long distances more efficiently than direct current. The so-called War of the Currents between the two systems was eventually won by alternating current, which remains the basis of most electrical grids today.

Coastal Erosion

Coastal erosion, the gradual wearing away of land along shorelines by the action of waves, tides, currents, and storms, is a natural process that has shaped coastlines for millions of years. In recent decades, however, concern has grown that human activity and climate change are accelerating erosion in many parts of the world, threatening homes, infrastructure, and ecosystems.

Waves erode coasts in several ways. Hydraulic action occurs when waves force air and water into cracks in rock, gradually widening them. Abrasion happens when waves hurl sand and pebbles against cliffs, grinding them down. Over time, these processes can carve out features such as caves, arches, and stacks, and cause cliffs to retreat inland, sometimes by more than a meter in a single year in areas of soft rock.

Several factors influence how quickly a coast erodes. Rock type is crucial: hard rocks such as granite resist erosion for long periods, while soft materials such as clay and sandstone can crumble rapidly. Wave energy, the shape of the coastline, and the presence of protective features such as beaches, dunes, and coastal vegetation also play important roles, as these absorb wave energy before it reaches the land.

Human activity can intensify erosion. Dams and river engineering reduce the amount of sediment reaching the coast, starving beaches of the sand that normally replenishes them. Removing mangroves and dunes for development removes natural barriers. Meanwhile, rising sea levels caused by global warming allow waves to attack higher up the shore and make storm surges more damaging.

Communities use a range of strategies to respond. Hard engineering approaches include sea walls, groynes, and rock barriers, which can protect specific locations but are expensive and sometimes shift erosion to neighboring areas. Soft approaches, such as beach nourishment and restoring dunes or wetlands, work more with natural processes. A growing number of planners also advocate managed retreat, in which buildings are relocated away from the most vulnerable shorelines rather than defended indefinitely.

PASSAGE 3

The Science of Dreams

Dreams have fascinated humans throughout history, interpreted variously as messages from gods, glimpses of the future, or windows into the hidden desires of the mind. Modern science, while still far from a complete explanation, has uncovered much about when and how dreams occur and what functions they might serve.

Most vivid dreaming takes place during rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep, a stage identified in the 1950s in which the brain becomes highly active while most of the body's muscles are temporarily paralyzed. This paralysis prevents people from physically acting out their dreams. Adults typically pass through four to six sleep cycles per night, with REM periods growing longer toward morning, which is why people often wake from dreams.

Several theories attempt to explain why we dream. The activation-synthesis hypothesis proposes that dreams result from the brain's attempt to make sense of random neural activity during REM sleep. Other researchers emphasize memory consolidation, suggesting that dreaming helps sort and store the day's experiences. A further view holds that dreams serve an emotional function, helping people process difficult feelings in a safe setting.

Dream content tends to reflect waking life. Studies of thousands of dream reports show that people commonly dream about familiar people, places, and concerns, and that emotions such as anxiety are more common than joy. Certain themes, including being chased, falling, or arriving unprepared for an exam, appear across many cultures, though their meanings are debated and are unlikely to be as fixed as popular dream dictionaries suggest.

A fascinating phenomenon is lucid dreaming, in which the dreamer becomes aware that they are dreaming and can sometimes influence the dream's course. Laboratory studies have confirmed lucid dreaming by asking trained sleepers to signal with pre-agreed eye movements. Researchers are exploring whether lucid dreaming techniques might help treat conditions such as recurring nightmares, although more evidence is needed.
