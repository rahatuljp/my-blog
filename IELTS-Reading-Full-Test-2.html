PASSAGE 1

The Discovery of Penicillin

In 1928, Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming made one of the most important medical discoveries in history—almost by accident. Fleming was researching bacteria at St. Mary's Hospital in London when he left for a two-week holiday, leaving several bacterial cultures uncovered in his laboratory. Upon his return, he noticed that one of the dishes had been contaminated by a blue-green mold, and strangely, the bacteria surrounding the mold had been destroyed.

Rather than simply discarding the contaminated dish, Fleming decided to investigate further. He identified the mold as belonging to the genus Penicillium and discovered that it produced a substance capable of killing a wide range of harmful bacteria. He named this substance penicillin. Fleming published his findings in 1929, but at the time, the scientific community showed relatively little interest, partly because producing penicillin in large enough quantities for medical use seemed extremely difficult.

It was not until a decade later that penicillin's true potential began to be realized. In the late 1930s, a team of scientists at Oxford University—led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain—took up the challenge of purifying and mass-producing the drug. Their work was accelerated by the urgent medical needs of the Second World War, during which infections from battlefield wounds killed enormous numbers of soldiers. By 1941, the Oxford team had successfully tested penicillin on human patients, with remarkable results.

Mass production posed a significant challenge, as European factories were focused on wartime manufacturing. The task of scaling up production shifted largely to the United States, where pharmaceutical companies developed new fermentation techniques that dramatically increased penicillin yields. By 1944, enough penicillin was being produced to treat all Allied soldiers wounded during the D-Day invasion.

Fleming, Florey, and Chain jointly received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945 for their contributions. Penicillin is widely regarded as the beginning of the modern antibiotic era, and it is estimated to have saved hundreds of millions of lives since its introduction. However, Fleming himself warned early on that overusing the drug could lead to bacteria developing resistance—a concern that has become an increasingly serious global health issue today.

Solar Power and the Renewable Energy Shift

As concerns about climate change and fossil fuel dependency continue to grow, solar power has emerged as one of the fastest-growing sources of renewable energy worldwide. Unlike coal or natural gas, solar energy is generated by converting sunlight directly into electricity, producing no direct emissions during operation.

The basic technology behind solar power, the photovoltaic (PV) cell, was first developed in the 1950s, but for decades it remained too expensive for widespread use, limited mainly to specialized applications such as powering satellites. Over the past two decades, however, the cost of manufacturing solar panels has fallen dramatically, driven by improvements in materials, manufacturing efficiency, and economies of scale as production increased globally.

Large-scale solar installations, often called solar farms or mega solar plants, now generate electricity for entire regions. These facilities consist of thousands, sometimes millions, of individual panels arranged across large areas of land, carefully engineered to maximize exposure to sunlight throughout the day. Engineers and technicians play a crucial role in designing the electrical layouts and wiring systems that connect these panels safely and efficiently to the power grid.

Despite its advantages, solar power faces certain challenges. Because it only generates electricity when the sun is shining, solar plants must be paired with energy storage systems, such as large batteries, or connected to a broader grid that includes other energy sources, to ensure a consistent power supply. Additionally, large solar farms require significant amounts of land, which can sometimes create conflicts with agricultural use or natural habitats.

Nevertheless, many countries have set ambitious targets for expanding solar capacity as part of broader efforts to reduce carbon emissions. Falling costs, combined with improving battery technology, suggest that solar power's share of global electricity generation will likely continue to grow substantially in the coming decades.

PASSAGE 3

Ocean Plastic Pollution

Plastic pollution in the world's oceans has become one of the most visible and widely discussed environmental issues of the 21st century. It is estimated that around eight million tonnes of plastic waste enter the ocean every year, originating from sources ranging from littered packaging to fishing equipment lost or abandoned at sea.

Unlike organic waste, most plastics do not biodegrade in any meaningful timeframe; instead, they break down into increasingly small fragments known as microplastics, which can persist in the environment for hundreds of years. These microplastics have been found virtually everywhere scientists have looked—from the deepest ocean trenches to Arctic sea ice, and even within the tissues of fish and other marine animals.

The impact on marine life is significant and well-documented. Larger pieces of plastic debris can entangle animals such as seals, turtles, and seabirds, sometimes causing injury or death. Smaller fragments are frequently mistaken for food, and when ingested, they can cause internal blockages or a false sense of fullness that leads to malnutrition, particularly in seabirds.

One especially concerning phenomenon is the accumulation of floating plastic debris in ocean gyres—large systems of rotating ocean currents that draw in and concentrate floating waste. The best-known example, the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, located between Hawaii and California, is estimated to cover an area several times the size of France, though it consists not of a solid mass but rather a diffuse concentration of plastic fragments spread across a vast area of ocean.

Addressing ocean plastic pollution requires action at multiple levels. Some countries have introduced bans or taxes on single-use plastic items such as bags and straws, while others have invested in improved waste management infrastructure to prevent plastic from entering waterways in the first place. Meanwhile, scientists and engineers continue developing new technologies aimed at removing existing plastic from the ocean, as well as biodegradable alternatives to conventional plastic materials.
