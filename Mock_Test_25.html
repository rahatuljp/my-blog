The History of Clocks

Humans have measured time for thousands of years, long before the mechanical clock existed. The earliest devices relied on natural phenomena: sundials, used in ancient Egypt and Babylon, tracked the sun's shadow to divide the day into parts. Their obvious limitation was that they were useless at night and on cloudy days.

To measure time regardless of weather, ancient civilizations developed water clocks, or clepsydrae, in which water flowed at a steady rate from one vessel into another, with markings showing how much time had passed. Used in Egypt, Greece, and China, water clocks were improved over centuries, and Chinese engineers in the 11th century built elaborate astronomical clock towers driven by water power.

The first mechanical clocks appeared in Europe in the late 13th and early 14th centuries, driven by falling weights and regulated by a device called the verge escapement. These early clocks, installed in church towers and town squares, were large and often inaccurate, losing or gaining as much as an hour a day, but they began to organize the daily life of towns around fixed hours.

A leap in accuracy came in 1656, when Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens built the first practical pendulum clock, inspired by Galileo's observation that a pendulum swings at a regular rate. Pendulum clocks reduced errors to less than a minute a day. In the 18th century, English clockmaker John Harrison constructed highly accurate marine chronometers that allowed sailors to determine longitude at sea, solving one of the great navigational problems of the age.

In the 20th century, quartz clocks, which use the steady vibrations of a quartz crystal, made accurate timekeeping cheap and widely available. Today the most accurate timekeepers are atomic clocks, which measure the vibrations of atoms and are so precise that they would lose less than a second over many millions of years. These clocks underpin technologies such as GPS and the synchronization of global communications networks.

The Rainforest Canopy

Tropical rainforests are often described as layered ecosystems, and the most biologically active of these layers is the canopy, the roof of interlocking tree crowns that forms roughly 30 to 45 meters above the ground. Until relatively recently, the canopy was one of the least explored habitats on Earth, as scientists lacked safe ways to reach it.

Researchers developed a variety of techniques to study this elevated world, including climbing ropes, suspended walkways, cranes, and even airships and balloons that float gently over the treetops. These methods revealed that the canopy teems with life: it is estimated that a large proportion of all rainforest species live in the upper layers and may never descend to the forest floor.

Life in the canopy is shaped by abundant sunlight, which fuels the production of flowers, fruit, and leaves on which many animals depend. Plants called epiphytes, including orchids and bromeliads, grow on tree branches rather than in soil, collecting water and nutrients from rain and falling debris. Some bromeliads hold small pools of water in their leaves, creating tiny aquatic habitats where frogs lay eggs and insects develop.

Many canopy animals show remarkable adaptations. Monkeys, sloths, and tree frogs have limbs or toe pads suited to gripping branches, while gliding animals such as flying squirrels and gliding lizards travel between trees without touching the ground. Many species of insects and birds are specialized to feed on particular canopy plants, making their survival closely tied to the health of the trees.

The canopy also has global importance. Through photosynthesis and the release of water vapor, canopy trees influence local rainfall and help regulate the climate, while the vast quantity of carbon stored in rainforest trees makes their protection a key part of efforts to limit climate change. Deforestation, which removes the canopy and all the life it supports, therefore threatens biodiversity and the climate alike.

PASSAGE 3

The Economics of Tourism

Tourism is one of the world's largest and fastest-growing industries, accounting for roughly one in ten jobs globally and generating a significant share of economic output. For many small countries and island nations, it is the single most important source of income and foreign currency, making it a powerful tool for economic development.

The economic benefits of tourism extend well beyond hotels and airlines. Visitors spend money on food, transport, souvenirs, and entertainment, supporting a wide range of local businesses. Economists describe this as a multiplier effect: money spent by tourists circulates through the local economy as businesses pay wages and buy supplies from other local firms, generating more income than the initial spending alone.

However, tourism also brings economic risks. Because it depends on factors such as exchange rates, safety, weather, and global events, tourism income can be highly unstable. Destinations that rely heavily on a single industry are particularly vulnerable, as the sudden collapse in international travel during global emergencies has shown. Many jobs in tourism are also seasonal and relatively low-paid.

A further concern is leakage, in which a large share of tourist spending leaves the destination rather than benefiting local people. This occurs, for example, when resorts are owned by foreign companies, when imported food and goods are used instead of local products, or when profits are sent abroad. In some developing countries, estimates suggest that a major proportion of tourist spending may leak out in this way.

Tourism can also impose costs on local communities and environments, including rising property prices, overcrowding, pollution, and damage to natural and cultural sites. In response, some destinations have introduced visitor caps, tourist taxes, or promoted sustainable tourism models that encourage visitors to stay longer, spend locally, and respect the environment, aiming to ensure that the benefits of tourism are shared more fairly with host communities.
